*Fifty years ago, Morocco invaded my country with 25,000 soldiers and 300,000 civilians. A few days later, Spain relinquished its mandate under international law to decolonise my country when it split the territory between Morocco and Mauritania, who later withdrew. Ever since, my people have been trying to warn you what happens when you let an occupation continue with impunity just because the great powers find it convenient.*

*This week, the warning arrived closer to the imperial core—and deadlier—than ever through the crisis at the Ceuta border. Don't say we didn't warn you.*

On 30 July 2026, the border between Morocco and the Spanish enclave of Ceuta effectively collapsed. Spanish officials put the number of irregular crossings within 24 hours as high as 50,000—in a city of roughly 85,000 residents—with the death toll climbing around 70 within days. Troops were deployed. Italy suspended Schengen cooperation, and others threatened to follow. Madrid was, once again, blindsided by a neighbour that had simply decided to stop enforcing a border.

This is not the first time, and the repetition should worry you. In May 2021, over 8,000 people crossed into Ceuta in two days after Rabat simply removed its own border controls. Why? Spain had allowed Sahrawi president Brahim Ghali to be treated for COVID-19 in a hospital in La Rioja without telling Morocco. The Makhzen—the name for the royal entourage that controls every aspect of Moroccan governance—sent an unambiguous message. Recognise our occupation of Western Sahara as legitimate, or we open the tap.

Five years and one much larger crisis later, the lesson still hasn't landed in Europe: Western Sahara is not a remote, low-stakes territorial dispute confined to a barely populated strip of Atlantic desert. It is the *raison d'être* of Morocco's foreign policy. Rabat has built an entire diplomatic strategy around trading recognition of its occupation for cooperation on almost anything else— and punishing whoever declines. Sweden had to walk back its recognition of the Sahrawi Republic in 2015 just to guarantee the opening of Morocco's first IKEA store. Ceuta's 2026 crisis is what that leverage looks like when a border security system the EU chose to outsource is pulled harder than ever before.

And it's being pulled harder because the reward for pulling it keeps getting bigger. The biggest culprits are the United States of Donald Trump and the Israel of Benjamin Netanyahu.

In 2020, Trump recognised Morocco's claim of sovereignty over Western Sahara as the reward for Morocco normalising relations with Israel under the Abraham Accords—making the US the first, and for years the only, state to do so. That recognition wasn't a one-off gesture from a departing administration. This same week, Trump reaffirmed it again in a message to King Mohammed VI marking the 27th anniversary of his accession to the throne, explicitly backing Morocco's so-called autonomy plan as the "only basis" for a solution and pledging deeper security and economic cooperation. Just days before that, Morocco named the 650-mile highway connecting the country to the coast of occupied Western Sahara after Trump, and he thanked the king for it.

Israel was the second country to formally recognise Moroccan so-called sovereignty over Western Sahara, in 2023, folding Rabat's territorial claim into the broader realignment of Israel-friendly states across the region. This is not a side effect of the Abraham Accords. It is one of the transactions the Accords were built on—Sahrawi self-determination quietly removed from the calculation of the Western powers meant to resolve the conflict in my homeland.

All of this is connected. It is the same genre of politics that recognised Israel's annexation of the Golan Heights and blessed West Bank settlements: unilateral recognition of a territorial fait accompli, dressed up through realpolitik as pragmatism, in open defiance of the UN's own classification of Western Sahara as a Non-Self-Governing Territory pending decolonisation—a classification the European Court of Justice has itself recently reaffirmed, ruling that Morocco and Western Sahara are legally distinct territories and that trade with the occupied territory is illegal without Sahrawi consent.

Europe's role here isn't innocent either. France has joined the US in recognising Moroccan claims of sovereignty outright, while Spain and others hide behind rhetorical support for the 2007 "autonomy" plan to keep doing business in the territory. And Spain's contradictions run deeper than Western Sahara alone: it broke ranks with Western diplomacy to oppose Israel's genocide in Gaza, under pressure from its own public opinion, and backs Ukraine's fight against a Russian invasion aimed at rebuilding an empire—yet it ignores its own historical responsibility as the colonial power in Western Sahara and stands in the way of Sahrawi self-determination. That is a moral contradiction built into Spain's selective application of the very international law it claims to champion. Now, Spain's defiance of the US and Israel over Gaza is being answered with a border crisis, backed by an alliance of American and Israeli interests perfectly happy to align with a monarchy that will support anyone who backs its claim to a Moroccan empire that never existed.

Stable relations with Rabat have always been sold to Europe as something you can buy: make a concession, get calm in return. What this week proves is that the calm was never bought. It was only ever rented, one concession at a time, and the bill comes due again whenever Rabat decides it should. Spain's concessions in 2021 didn't buy peace—they proved to an emboldened Makhzen that blackmail works.

It doesn't help that the global far-right is now building what looks like a contradictory relationship but is in fact symbiosis with Morocco. In Europe, Rabat's willingness to weaponise migration hands anti-immigration movements exactly the images they want: chaos at the border, towns overwhelmed, a government powerless against a "horde" of people it frames as illegitimate. The actual cause—a foreign government's leverage over an unresolved colonial dispute—goes largely undiscussed. And globally, every unpunished annexation is a precedent banked for the next one: normalise this, and you have told every state watching that territorial law is negotiable, provided you have the right allies and the right timing.

This week's crisis wasn't a migrant crisis. It was a new stage in Morocco's hybrid warfare, waged to advance the interests of its own monarchy, and fought using Moroccan youth who are disappointed with their own politics and economy, hopeless about their country's future, and desperate to leave. Sahrawis have been warning about this for decades, not as abstract solidarity but as forecast: unresolved occupation doesn't stay contained. It leaks into migration policy, into regional alliances, into the credibility of every international law you claim to believe in.

We told you. We are still telling you. The only question left is whether you are finally listening.
